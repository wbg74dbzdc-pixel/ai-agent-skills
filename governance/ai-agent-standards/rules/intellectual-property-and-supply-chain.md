# Intellectual Property and Supply Chain

Use this rule to establish rights and reduce avoidable infringement risk. It is issue-spotting, not legal advice; ownership, transfer, moral rights, and enforceability vary by jurisdiction and facts.

## Provenance and ownership

- Maintain a rights record for every material contribution: code, art, animation, models, music, voice, writing, game design, levels, fonts, data, tools, and other assets. Record creator, source, date/version, applicable license or agreement, permitted uses, required notices/attribution, restrictions, and evidence location.
- Distinguish project ownership from a license to use something. Repository access, payment, possession of source files, a purchase, or a “work made for hire” label alone may not establish ownership. Verify the actual agreement and applicable law.
- Before accepting work from employees, contractors, co-developers, publishers, collaborators, or contributors, document who owns pre-existing materials and new work, which rights are assigned or licensed, permitted project and commercial uses, payment/credit, further permissions, and delivery of editable/source files. If the project owner is meant to own new work, use an effective written transfer/assignment to that owner to the extent allowed by applicable law. If the contributor retains ownership, treat the work as licensed rather than owned and deliberately confirm the license covers the required uses. Obtain qualified legal review for material contributions and cross-border work.
- Do not assume commissioned work automatically belongs to the commissioning party or qualifies as work made for hire. For example, U.S. work-made-for-hire rules are limited; confirm the legal requirements rather than relying on a label.
- Track open-source, marketplace, stock, commissioned, public-domain, and third-party assets separately. Confirm license compatibility with distribution, monetization, modification, platform, and sublicensing needs; preserve notices and meet source-offer or attribution obligations.
- For AI-assisted or generated material, record the provider/tool, applicable terms and date, meaningful human contributions, and any source material or assets supplied. Do not promise exclusive ownership or copyright protection for output without checking current law, tool terms, and the facts.

## Product terms, platforms, and user content

- Review the actual current EULA/terms of service, privacy policy, store/publisher agreement, SDK terms, and user-content/mod/workshop rules for every release channel. Record the version/date and the obligations they impose on the developer and users.
- Keep the product owner's rights in the game and its original assets distinct from the limited license players receive to access and use the product. Do not use terms that purport to transfer ownership of the game or players' content by accident.
- For uploads, mods, fan content, feedback, and other user contributions, state who owns the contribution and grant only the permissions the service actually needs. Define scope, purpose, media, territory, duration, sublicensing, modification/derivative-work rights, removal, and continued distribution after removal. Broad, irrevocable, perpetual, transferable, sublicensable, exclusive, or commercial grants require deliberate scope review; these words are not automatically invalid, but can have major consequences.
- Check platform terms separately: publishing may require a license to host, reproduce, distribute, display, moderate, adapt for compatibility, or sublicense submitted content. Explain that effect accurately before enabling uploads. Do not promise that a contributor retains unrestricted control if platform or in-game terms grant broader rights.
- For mods and workshop content, address derivative works, game IP, compatibility, redistribution, monetization, support, takedowns, and whether the mod may be used or adapted in the game. Do not imply users can commercialize mods or that the developer owns them unless the governing rights support that statement.
- Preserve dated copies of accepted terms, platform terms, contributor agreements, permissions, notices, and release evidence.

## Clearance and risk controls

- Never assume public availability, search visibility, AI generation, or absence of a watermark grants usage rights.
- Confirm dependency and asset licenses are compatible with the intended distribution and business model. Scan dependencies for known vulnerabilities, abandoned packages, suspicious maintainers, typosquatting, and unnecessary transitive risk.
- Pin versions and verify integrity where practical; review lockfile and dependency changes. Produce and retain an SBOM when risk, regulation, customers, deployment context, or incident response needs justify it.
- Identify trademark, publicity, patent, privacy, dataset, model, and training-data questions that require qualified review rather than inventing clearance.
- Maintain a replacement or removal path for third-party components and assets, and a process to receive, assess, and respond to rights complaints.
- No checklist or terms can guarantee that work cannot be copied, prevent every claim, or eliminate lawsuit risk. Reduce avoidable risk with documented rights, accurate terms, appropriate review, and timely response; escalate high-impact questions to qualified counsel.

Primary references: U.S. Copyright Office, [Circular 30: Works Made for Hire](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf), [Copyright Act, Chapter 2: Copyright Ownership and Transfer](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap2.html), and [Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf); Steam, [Subscriber Agreement, including Workshop provisions](https://store.steampowered.com/subscriber_agreement/). These are jurisdiction- or platform-specific starting points, not universal rules. Check the current text that applies to the actual project.
